## ERIC abstract (public record)

Most students, perhaps over 90 percent, can master what teachers have to teach them, and it is the task of instruction to find the means which will enable students to master the subject. A basic task is to determine what is meant by mastery and to search for methods and materials which will enable the largest proportion of students to attain it. To promote mastery learning, five variables must be dealt with: (1) aptitude, viewed as time required to attain mastery; (2) quality of instruction, approaching the optimum for a given learner; (3) ability to understand instruction; (4) perseverance, time one is willing to spend; and (5) time allowed for learning, the key to mastery.

---

## The problem Bloom opens with

Teachers, he writes, begin a term expecting about a third of students to learn adequately, a third to fail or “get by,” and a third to learn a good deal but not be “good students.” Grading policy transmits that expectation and becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy.

He calls that sorting “the most wasteful and destructive aspect of the present educational system.” It lowers aspiration, kills motivation, and “systematically destroys the ego and self-concept” of students who are legally required to attend for 10–12 years.

The thesis:

> Most students (perhaps over 90 percent) can master what we have to teach them, and it is the task of instruction to find the means which will enable our students to master the subject under consideration.

The job is to **define mastery** and find methods so the largest proportion can reach it. Individual differences stay real; they should not be turned into a ceiling on what each person is allowed to learn.

## Against the normal curve

Achievement tests are often built to detect even trivial differences, then grades are forced onto a bell curve. Failures one year may perform like C students another year; A students in one school may match F students in another.

Bloom: there is nothing sacred about the normal curve. It is the distribution of **chance**. Education is purposeful. If instruction works, the achievement distribution should **not** look normal. An outcome that still looks like a normal curve is evidence the teaching failed.

“Individual differences” are facts. Using them as the *standard* of success is a policy choice, not a necessity.

## Carroll’s model (the theoretical spine)

Bloom builds the strategy on Carroll, J. (1963). A model of school learning. *Teachers College Record, 64*, 723–733.

Carroll’s short form, as Bloom restates it:

- If students are normally distributed on **aptitude**, and everyone gets the **same** instruction (same amount, quality, and time), achievement will be normal and the aptitude–achievement correlation will be high (he says +.70 or more if measures are valid).
- If aptitude is still normally distributed, but **kind and quality of instruction and time** are fitted to each student, **most students can reach mastery** and the aptitude–achievement correlation should approach **zero**.

That second paragraph is the whole paper.

Also cited as background: Morrison (1926), Bruner (1966), Skinner (1954), Suppes (1966), Goodlad & Anderson (1959), Glaser (1968).

## Five variables

### 1. Aptitude for particular kinds of learning

Common view: aptitude tests predict achievement, so only the most able can reach high levels.

Carroll’s view, which Bloom adopts: **aptitude is the time required to reach mastery of a task.** Given enough time, mastery is theoretically available to all.

Support he cites: grade norms (today’s top scorers are tomorrow’s typical scorers); self-paced studies where almost everyone masters, but at different speeds (Glaser, 1968; Atkinson, 1967).

He does **not** say everyone is identical:

- Top 1–5%: possible special talent (fluency others do not have).
- Bottom ~5%: real disabilities for a given subject (tone deafness for music, etc.). Share varies by subject.
- The middle ~90%: aptitude predicts **rate**, not the **level** of complexity they can reach.

His working claim: given time and appropriate help, **about 95%** (top 5% + next 90%) can reach a high mastery level — the grade of A as an index of mastery, “under appropriate conditions.”

Some students may need years where others need a fraction of a year. If the time cost is prohibitive, mastery learning has failed its own test. The practical problem is **shrinking time for slower students**, not lowering the bar.

Aptitude is not completely stable (he cites Bloom, 1964, *Stability and Change in Human Characteristics*, and Hunt, 1961). Early home and elementary years matter most; some change is still possible later. Even without changing aptitude, better conditions can cut time-to-mastery.

### 2. Quality of instruction

Schools assume one standard classroom (he uses a 1:30 teacher–student ratio and “the best method for the group”). Carroll defines quality as how close presentation, explanation, and ordering come to the **optimum for a given learner**.

Same objectives can be learned by different routes: independent study vs highly structured teaching (Congreve, 1965); more examples; more repetition; more reinforcement.

A good **tutor** searches for the quality (and motivation) that student needs. Bloom reports an unpublished algebra study: students getting as much home tutoring as class time showed almost **zero** correlation between math aptitude and year-end achievement; students with only class instruction showed about **+.90**. The extra help was the quality those students needed.

Quality is judged by **effect on the individual**, not on a random group. He does not assume one tutor per child as a school system — research should improve group instruction too.

### 3. Ability to understand instruction

Defined as: ability to understand **the nature of the task** and **the procedures to follow**.

In verbal classrooms this is largely **verbal ability and reading comprehension**. Those measures, he says, correlate about +.50 to +.60 with high-school/college GPA and with achievement in most subjects.

You can improve verbal ability more at preschool and elementary ages (Bloom, 1964); vocabulary and reading can still move later, with diminishing returns.

Immediate payoff is **changing the instruction**, not waiting to change the student:

- Small cooperative groups (two or three) to work difficulties without competitive harm.
- Tutorial help **one-to-one**, preferably someone other than the classroom teacher, used when cheaper alternatives fail; the tutor should leave the student less dependent, not more.
- Alternative textbooks, workbooks, programmed units, film, lab materials, games — not a different curriculum for the whole term, but **another explanation at the point of stuck**.

If one way fails, another must be available. That fights “defeatism and passivity.”

### 4. Perseverance

Carroll: time the learner is **willing** to spend (distinct from clock time present). If needed time is T and the student spends less, mastery does not happen.

Perseverance varies by person and by task (a student who quits algebra may persist on a car engine). Success and frequent reward increase it; frustration cuts it.

Bloom’s research note: the **demand** for perseverance drops when materials fit and when **frequent feedback plus specific help** is given. Better explanations reduce how much grit is required.

He is blunt: endurance is a virtue in long-distance running; it is not the point of school. “The emphasis should be on learning, not on vague ideas of discipline and endurance.”

### 5. Time allowed for learning

“For Carroll, the time spent on learning is the key to mastery.” Aptitude sets rate; the student must both spend the time needed and **be allowed** that time.

Fixed terms mean too much time for some, too little for others. Homework hours are a weak predictor of achievement (Husén, 1967: about −.05 to +.05 in the international math study). It is not sheer hours.

A strategy must change **how much time each student needs** (better instruction) and **how time is provided**. He hopes the slower:faster time ratio can move from about **6:1** toward **3:1**.

## One operating strategy (Chicago group)

They kept the usual group course, calendar, and materials, and **added** diagnosis plus alternatives so most students could hit a **predetermined standard** inside the regular term. They started with courses that have few prerequisites (algebra, science). Late-sequence subjects with years of accumulated failure are harder to remediate in one term.

### Preconditions

1. **Define mastery** so you can recognize it. Specify objectives and content; translate them into evaluation procedures.
2. Separate **teaching–learning** from **summative evaluation**. Both teacher and learner need the achievement criteria and evidence of progress.
3. Prefer **absolute (criterion) standards** over rank-order competition. It is conceivable that everyone earns A. It is also possible that in a given year nobody does — if the standard is real.
4. National standards are **not** what he recommends. He wants **realistic local performance standards**, then instruction that lets the majority reach them.
5. In one course they reused 1965 cut scores on a parallel 1966 exam and told students they were not competing with each other. Cooperation followed.

Grades as relative rank make students hide help. Criterion grades make help safe.

### Operating procedures

Keep the teacher’s usual methods. Supplement them.

- Break the course into **units**.
- After each unit, a short **diagnostic-progress test**. Bloom takes the term **formative evaluation** from Scriven (1967).
- Formative tests **pace** learning and show what is still unmastered.
- Mark them **mastery / nonmastery** — he argues they should **not** be graded. A grade on a diagnostic test turns it back into a sorter.
- Pair every nonmastery result with a **specific prescription**: pages in another text, a workbook unit, a programmed sequence, AV, a small group, a tutor.
- Use the same formative items year to year as **quality control** on the instruction itself (he describes 1966 vs 1967 unit comparisons).

No single material is indispensable. Variety tells the student: if this way failed, another way exists.

### Outcomes he cares about (not only scores)

When students get objective and subjective evidence of mastery:

- **Interest** in the subject rises (“like” it, want more). Failure closes a field.
- **Self-concept**: people treat themselves as adequate where they get proof of competence. Frequent public recognition of mastery is, he says, a mental-health intervention. Chronic school failure is the opposite.
- **Lifelong learning**: if school is only frustration, later learning is rejected. “It is this continual learning which should be the major goal of the educational system.”

Mastery must be both the student’s private sense of competence **and** public certification. Denying public recognition while the student has in fact learned teaches the student that *they* are inadequate, not the grading system.

## What this is not

- Keller’s **Personalized System of Instruction** (PSI, 1968) is a sibling, not the same paper. Bloom’s model is group instruction **plus** feedback and correctives, not a pure go-at-your-own-pace module system (see later writing by Thomas Guskey, who studied with Bloom).
- It is not “everyone gets an A for effort.” The standard is defined in advance; time and path vary.
- It is not a WCAG technique. It is a way to teach a defined bar — including a 508 / Title II / 2.2-AA **profile** — without treating the first quiz as a permanent aptitude sort.

## Pairing with the taxonomy

*Learning for Mastery* tells you to name the criterion and keep teaching until it is met. The *Taxonomy* tells you **what kind of criterion** you named. “Master WCAG” is empty until you say whether mastery is Remember (list the 38 508 criteria), Apply (fix a form), or Evaluate (write a conformance claim).
